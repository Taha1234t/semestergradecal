Weighted vs. Unweighted Grades: What's the Difference and Why It Matters

August 10, 2026 · 6 min read

If you've ever compared report cards with a friend and realized your "A" doesn't seem to count the same as theirs, you've run into the difference between weighted and unweighted grades. It's one of the most misunderstood parts of the U.S. high school grading system — and understanding it can change how you think about which classes to take.

Here's exactly what separates the two, how each is calculated, and why colleges care.

What Are Unweighted Grades?

On an unweighted GPA scale, an A generally receives the same grade-point value regardless of whether it comes from a regular, Honors, AP, or other advanced course. There's no bonus for taking a harder course.

Unweighted GPAs commonly use a 0.0–4.0 scale, although grading systems can vary between schools:

Letter GradeUnweighted Value
A4.0
B3.0
C2.0
D1.0
F0.0

This system measures academic performance without adding extra GPA points for course difficulty. It answers a simple question: how well did you do in your classes? — without factoring in how hard those classes actually were.

What Are Weighted Grades?

A weighted GPA adjusts the grade-point value assigned to a course based on factors such as course level or difficulty. Honors, AP, IB, and dual-enrollment classes may be treated as more rigorous, and some schools award additional GPA points for grades earned in those courses.

Note: Weighted GPA scales vary by high school and school district. The 4.5 and 5.0 values shown below are examples of common weighting systems, not a universal U.S. standard.

Example of a Weighted GPA Scale

Here is an example of one weighted GPA scale:

Course LevelABC
Regular4.03.02.0
Honors4.53.52.5
AP / IB / Dual Enrollment5.04.03.0

On some grading scales, a weighted GPA can exceed 4.0 because schools may award additional GPA points for grades earned in more rigorous courses such as AP, IB, or Honors classes. As NACAC explains, many high schools report both a weighted and an unweighted GPA, with weighted GPAs awarding additional points for rigorous coursework such as Honors, AP, and IB classes.

The Core Difference, in One Sentence

Unweighted GPAs measure your grades without additional points for course difficulty. Weighted GPAs account for both your grades and the level of coursework you took.

Neither one is "wrong" — they're just answering different questions.

A Side-by-Side Example

Imagine two students, both earning an A in one class:

  • Student A takes a regular-level English class and earns an A → unweighted value: 4.0, weighted value: 4.0
  • Student B takes AP English and earns an A → unweighted value: 4.0, while the weighted value may be higher depending on the school's grading scale.

Same letter grade. Same "A" on the report card. But Student B may receive more GPA points because the course is weighted differently by the high school.

Now zoom out to a full course load:

Same grades. Different story, depending on which scale you're reading.

Keep in mind, depending on the high school, semester grades may be combined to calculate a cumulative GPA — and the way individual grades combine into that final number brings up its own set of questions. Why Do Schools Weight Grades at All?

One purpose of weighted grading is to recognize the additional rigor of more challenging courses. Schools may award extra GPA points for courses such as Honors, AP, or IB classes. This means students taking more rigorous courses may receive additional GPA points for the same letter grade — an A in AP Chemistry reflects a different level of course difficulty than an A in a standard-level equivalent, and weighted grading tries to capture that.

Do Weighted or Unweighted Grades Matter More for College Admissions?

This is where most of the confusion — and anxiety — comes from. The honest answer: it depends on the school you're applying to.

College Board notes that colleges consider a range of factors in admissions decisions, including the specific courses a student took and the grades they earned, and that many colleges look favorably on students who challenge themselves with rigorous coursework such as AP classes.

  • Some colleges may recalculate GPA or use their own methodology when reviewing applicants, because high schools use different grading scales.
  • Many admissions offices evaluate grades and course rigor in context, considering what challenging coursework was available to the student.

In practice, this means chasing a specific weighted number is usually less useful than focusing on two things: taking the most challenging classes reasonably available to you, and keeping your grades strong (or trending upward) across those classes.

A Few Things Worth Knowing

  • A weighted GPA above 4.0 doesn't mean you're "smarter." It reflects course difficulty, not raw ability — two students with very different weighted numbers can be equally capable, just enrolled in different course levels.
  • A strong grade in a challenging course can demonstrate academic rigor, but colleges evaluate grades and course selection in the context of the student's overall transcript. College Board specifically notes that highly selective colleges look for students who take more challenging classes and perform well in them.
  • Not all schools weight the same way. The point bonus for honors or AP classes (0.5, 1.0, or something else entirely) varies by school district, so weighted grades aren't directly comparable between schools.
  • Course rigor can be an important part of how colleges evaluate a student's academic record, rather than looking only at the raw GPA number.

The Bottom Line

Weighted and unweighted grades aren't competing systems — they're two lenses on the same performance. Unweighted grades show consistency, while weighted grades show consistency plus the level of challenge you took on. Colleges consider students' grades and the rigor of the courses they take, although the way they evaluate GPA can vary by institution.
